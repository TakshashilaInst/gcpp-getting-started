How the twelve weeks are structured

Everyone in the cohort studies the same three core modules for the first four weeks. Week 5 is the Takshashila Academic Conference, which closes the core curriculum. From Week 6 the cohort splits and you follow your own specialisation for seven weeks, ending in a workshop.

Assessment differs by specialisation and nothing else does. Advanced Public Policy and Technology and Policy are graded on published work and a final assignment; Defence and Foreign Affairs is graded on two quizzes. The switcher above sets the page to yours.

  • Weeks 1–4. Three core modules, taken by the whole cohort, whatever the specialisation.
  • Week 5. The Takshashila Academic Conference, which closes the core curriculum. Two days, both compulsory.
  • Break week. No webinars. Defence and Foreign Affairs only.
  • Weeks 6–11. Three specialisation modules, plus midweek guest sessions.
  • Week 12. Exercise AMRUT, online, over three days. Compulsory.
  • Assessment. Two quizzes and nothing else. Quiz 1 after the conference, Quiz 2 after Exercise AMRUT.

  • Be punctual. We value timeliness highly at Takshashila and will begin all sessions on time.
  • Do not interrupt others during webinars; wait for your turn to speak.

  • Often, students will be a part of candid conversations. Maintain confidentiality if any faculty, student, or guest requests it.
  • Maintain a convivial, collegial atmosphere.

1 · Public engagement — 50%

This component runs alongside the webinars from now until roughly two weeks after the final webinar, so you can apply what you are learning as you learn it. It has three parts.

Policy outputs — what each one is worth

Points, capped at 10 · any combination

  • Op-ed10
  • Conference talk5
  • Podcast4
  • Class debate — persuaded4
  • Blog post2
  • Class debate — participation2

Points reflect the effort and the editorial gatekeeping each format demands. One op-ed reaches the cap on its own; five blog posts do the same. Going past 10 is welcome but is not scored higher.

What makes something a policy output

Policy analysis. Not an opinion, not a reflection on the week, not a conference you attended — an argument that examines a policy, weighs what exists against what could exist, and reaches somewhere. A blog gives you freedom over how much of a problem you bite off; an op-ed needs a tighter structure and a clear recommendation at the end.

Where it can be published

Blog posts: Substack, LinkedIn, Medium, Blogspot or your own site. Op-eds: a recognised news outlet — a daily, a digital platform, a weekly magazine or a newsletter. Podcasts: audio or video, on Spotify, Apple Podcasts or a channel of your own, with at least two people speaking. Vernacular writing is welcome.

If you cannot publish

Some employers restrict public writing. If yours does, send your blog post in as an unpublished document and it will be considered. An op-ed will not work in that case, since the outlet is part of what it is.

Technology and Policy students have a second route: the in-class debates. Two points for taking part, four if the room votes your way.

2 · Final assignment — 50%

An individual submission, released after the final webinar in Week 12, with about two weeks to work on it. You choose between two problems: formulate a policy solution or analyse a policy or bill using frameworks from the programme.

The written submission is worth half and the viva the other half. The viva is a ten to fifteen minute conversation with an evaluator and three or four of your peers about your own submission — your problem statement, the market failures you identified, your key takeaways, and where the work could go next. It is deliberately informal.

Why there is a viva at all

Artificial intelligence is encouraged as a study tool and the programme will show you how to use it well. It is not a substitute for your own analysis. The viva exists so that understanding, rather than output, is what gets assessed — and because articulating a policy argument out loud is most of the job.

Academic integrity

The anti-plagiarism policy

Takshashila treats academic integrity as a prerequisite, not a formality. Collaboration is encouraged; reproducing someone else's work is not.

What counts as plagiarism

To plagiarise is to pass off another person's ideas or words as your own, to use their work without crediting the source, or to present something derived from an existing source as new and original. In practice that covers:

  • Turning in someone else's work as your own.
  • Copying words or ideas from someone else without giving credit.
  • Failing to put a quotation in quotation marks.
  • Giving incorrect information about the source of a quotation.
  • Changing the words but copying a source's sentence structure without giving credit.
  • Copying so many words or ideas from a source that they make up the majority of your work — whether or not you give credit.

Copying or reproducing another student's work for quizzes and assignments leads to disciplinary action for everyone involved.

Penalties

What happens if it is found

  • Squad activities. A grade penalty on the public engagement grade for every member of the group, not only the author.
  • Individual assignments. A grade of F for that assignment, and a transcript recording academic progress up to that point only.
  • Beyond this cohort. It may affect your prospects for admission to future Takshashila programmes.

How to cite

Citations build trust rather than diminishing it — they let a reader consult what you consulted. The house guidance:

SituationWhat to do
Paraphrasing an authorName the author and the year of publication in your text, and carry the full citation as a footnote: author name, paper or article title, publication, year.
Using an expert's ideaSuperscript a citation number where the idea appears and put the reference in a footnote at the foot of the page. Keep your own input as the crux of the piece rather than filling the page with references.
Research papersAuthor's name, the paper's name, and the page number of the content you used.
Electronic sourcesAuthor's name, the title, and the URL of the page you took the information from. Prefer short URLs. A research paper you read as a PDF was usually published elsewhere first, so it is not an electronic source.
Direct quotationsEnclose the author's words in quotation marks so the borrowed portion begins and ends visibly, and cite it in a footnote. Where you can, summarise in your own words instead — and still name the source.
Facts used as evidenceAsk whether the fact is common knowledge. If it is not, check that it is trustworthy and name the authoritative source.
Before you submit

Run your work through a plagiarism checker and read what it tells you. If a citation question falls outside this guidance, ask your instructor rather than guessing — the penalty for plagiarism is considerably higher than the penalty for a late submission.

Where AI fits

Takshashila publishes a Statement on the Use of AI ↗ setting out the principles the institution applies to its own research, teaching materials and publications. It governs Takshashila's output rather than student submissions, so it is not a set of rules you are graded against — but it is the clearest statement of the judgment the programme expects, and the four principles transfer directly to your own work.

The four principles
  • Accountability. You are responsible for your work whichever tools helped make it. AI is not an author or a co-creator.
  • Transparency. AI assistance is disclosed — a line as plain as "this essay was made with the assistance of AI" is the model.
  • Verification. Anything AI generates, sources included, is independently checked before you rely on it.
  • No substitution. AI supports the analysis; it does not replace it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Specialisation

I do not remember choosing a specialisation. Which am I in?

It is named in your acceptance email. A form to change specialisation, along with other details, will be circulated after the Academic Conference.

Can I switch specialisation?

Yes, at the Academic Conference in Week 5 — that is the point at which the cohort splits, and the conference tracks are organised around the three specialisations so you can get a feel for each before deciding.

One exception: armed forces personnel nominated by the defence services for Defence and Foreign Affairs cannot switch.

Can I do more than one specialisation?

Not within one cohort. You can take another in a future cohort, and the team will share how after you complete this one.

Attendance and recordings

How is attendance recorded?

It is not recorded for ordinary Saturday webinars. For the Academic Conference, attendance reports are pulled from Webex. Workshop attendance is recorded too.

Is attendance graded?

No — except that the Academic Conference and your workshop are compulsory for the certificate. Around 70% attendance is recommended: students who reach it tend to get more out of the programme and complete it.

Where do I find recordings and slides?

Open Takshashila → Learner's Guide → Resources. Sections are named after each webinar. Recordings go up 24 to 48 hours after the session, with all the readings hyperlinked alongside. Session slides are posted to Open Takshashila as well.

I am not getting the class emails.

Write to your specialisation mailbox with the address you registered under, and check your spam folder in the meantime. The Google Calendar link and the Open Takshashila invitation were both sent in the welcome email.

The readings look like a lot. How much is really expected?

Plan for five to six hours a week. Finish the mandatory readings; the recommended ones can come later. Coming in having understood the concept is enough — the webinars are instructive even if you start from zero.

Assessment and submissions

What is the pass mark out of 100?

There is not one. The certificate depends on completing every component of your specialisation. For Advanced Public Policy and Technology and Policy that means the public engagement requirements, the final assignment including the viva, the Academic Conference and the workshop. For Defence and Foreign Affairs it means both quizzes, the conference and Exercise AMRUT.

Are there assignments after each Saturday?

No. There are two to three squad activities across the programme, circulated by email and on Open Takshashila, and then the final assignment.

Are policy output points additive, or does only one submission count?

Additive, up to a cap of 10. One op-ed gets you there on its own. Five blog posts do the same. Any mix works. Producing more than 10 points' worth is welcome but does not score higher.

When are things due?
  • Community Organisation Exercise — around Week 10, early November. Briefed in Week 4.
  • Policy outputs — any time from now until roughly two weeks after the final webinar; expect a deadline in the first half of December, confirmed when the submission form goes out.
  • Final assignment — released after the final webinar on 21 November, with about two weeks to write it. First or second week of December.
Can I use AI for my work?

For learning, yes, and the programme will show you how — NotebookLM is built into the reading library. Not as a replacement for your own analysis. The final assignment is split between a written submission and a viva precisely so that what is assessed is your understanding rather than your output.

Takshashila's Statement on the Use of AI ↗ sets out the institution's own principles: accountability, disclosure, independent verification, and no substitution for your own thinking. Worth reading before you lean on a tool.

Public engagement

Will I get guidance, or am I on my own?

There is a dedicated session on writing for public policy, timed to land as public engagement opens. Prompts will be posted during the specialisation weeks to get you started.

Does my public engagement work have to stick to one theme?

No. Pick areas you are already interested in and apply what you are learning in the programme to them. Past cohorts have written on education, growth, climate and much else, across a mix of platforms.

My organisation will not let me publish. What then?

Send your blog post in as an unpublished document and it will be considered. An op-ed will not work, since publication in a recognised outlet is part of what makes it one. If you are in Technology and Policy, the in-class debates are a second route to the same points.

Does a podcast have to have video? Can it be an animated explainer?

No video needed — the only requirement is at least two people speaking. Animated explainers are not the standard format but can be considered. Propose it before you build it. You can set up your own podcast account and publish a discussion with people who share your interest.

Can a blog live on my own website rather than one of the listed platforms?

Yes — a personal Substack or a site of your own is fine, including something more elaborate like a data-driven piece. Only op-eds have to appear on a reasonably well-known news platform.

Can I write in a language other than English?

Yes. A vernacular op-ed counts.

How do the Technology and Policy debates work?

Six debates of about twenty minutes each on topical questions — whether social media should be banned for children, that kind of thing. Two debaters, the rest of the cohort as audience, questions, then a vote. They run under the Chatham House Rule and use the Purva Paksha format: you have to state your opponent's case at its strongest before making your own. Two points for taking part, four if you move the room.

Is the November workshop open to Defence and Foreign Affairs students?

Do Defence and Foreign Affairs students have to travel anywhere?

What are the Exercise AMRUT timings?

What if I cannot make all three days of my workshop?

Are these dates final?

Yes.

Academic integrity

How strictly is plagiarism handled?

Strictly. Citations and a bibliography are required on everything you submit. In a squad activity, plagiarism reflects on the entire squad. There are penalties in the final assignment. Run a plagiarism check on your own work before you submit it.

What is the difference between a citation and a mention?

A mention is where you refer to a source inside your text — in the paragraph, as you use it. A citation is the full entry in the list at the end, covering every page, paper and piece of literature you drew on. Both are expected.
